Gender and Emotion in Video Game Dialogue

Video games serve as a powerful medium for storytelling, drawing players into narratives shaped by character dialogues, attributes, and actions. Dialogue, in particular, plays a crucial role in conveying emotion, shaping how characters express themselves and interact within the game world. This visualization project explores how gender differences manifest in emotional expressions and verb usage in role-playing video game dialogue. By examining these patterns, the project aims to uncover potential disparities in how male and female characters are created, shedding light on broader cultural assumptions about gender and emotion in interactive media.

The motivation for this project arises from ongoing discussions about gender representation in gaming and media more broadly. Role-playing video games, in particular, offer deeply immersive experiences where players interact with characters through dialogue, shaping their perception of gender and emotion in virtual spaces. Since dialogue serves as a key mechanism for storytelling and character development, the way emotional expression is constructed across different genders can reveal underlying biases in game development. By analyzing patterns in emotional expression and verb usage, the project seeks to uncover how gender norms and stereotypes are reinforced or challenged within video game narratives. These findings not only contribute to discussions on diversity and inclusion in gaming but also offer insights into how interactive media reflects and perpetuates broader cultural attitudes about gender and emotion.

This project utilizes a dataset containing classified emotion labels and lemmatized verbs extracted from video game dialogues. By analyzing these features, the project explores whether male and female characters differ in the types of emotions they express and the verbs they use to convey their actions and feelings. This emotion analysis investigates whether there are systematic patterns in emotional expression that align with traditional gender norms. Additionally, examining verb usage can provide insights into why certain emotions are distributed disproportionately across genders. By identifying these trends, the project aims to contribute to a deeper understanding of how video game narratives reinforce or challenge existing cultural assumptions about gender and emotion. Moreover, the findings may offer valuable insights for game developers seeking to create more diverse and nuanced character portrayals.

Visualization 1: Frequency of Emotions

Method

To extract the most dominant emotion for each dialogue, a custom function, extract_dominant_emotions(), is implemented. This function utilizes max() to determine the emotion with the highest score for each dialogue entry. In addition, any instances labeled as “neutral” are excluded to focus on explicitly emotional expressions. Once the dominant emotions—anger, disgust, fear, joy, sadness, and surprise—are extracted, their counts are aggregated across all dialogue entries.

Plotly is used to generate this interactive bar chart that visualizes the frequency of emotions. The x-axis represents different emotion categories ordered in descending frequency, while the y-axis displays their respective counts. Each bar is colored according to a predefined mapping inspired by Inside Out. The layout() function is applied to refine various design elements, including title positioning, axis formatting, and font styles. The top margin is increased to accommodate the title without overlap, and all textual elements are styled with a serif font, consistent across all three visualizations. Additionally, the tickformat “,d” ensures that numerical values on the y-axis are formatted with commas. Interactive hover features are also enabled, allowing users to inspect precise emotion counts dynamically.

Result

The bar chart provides a clear quantitative breakdown of the different emotions expressed in video game dialogue, offering insights into which emotions are most and least frequently portrayed. The visualization reveals that surprise and anger are the most dominant emotions in the dataset, whereas sadness and fear appear far less frequently. This pattern suggests a narrative emphasis on high-intensity emotions, potentially reflecting the action-driven storytelling typical of role-playing video games.

The visualization maintains accuracy by faithfully representing the dataset, aggregating dominant emotions without distortion. Its functional design ensures that viewers can quickly extract meaningful insights, with emotions logically ordered by frequency. The distinct color coding for each emotion enhances categorization and readability, making it easy to differentiate emotional expressions at a glance. Beyond its functionality, the visualization’s effectiveness also lies in its clean and polished design. The bars are evenly spaced, and the color scheme aligns with common emotional associations, reinforcing intuitive interpretation. The choice of a serif font adds a formal touch, making the visualization well-suited for a research project. As an interactive plot, the visualization allows users to hover over each bar to view precise emotion counts. This interactive feature encourages deeper engagement, prompting audiences to consider whether this distribution aligns with real-world emotional expression or if it reflects creative biases embedded in game development. By examining these patterns, the visualization sparks discussions about how emotions are scripted in video game narratives and what this might reveal about broader trends in character development.

Visualization 2: Distribution of Emotions by Gender

Method

Similar to the first visualization, the function extract_dominant_emotions() is used to identify the most dominant emotion in each dialogue entry and count occurrences for each predefined emotion category. Another crucial step involves computing normalized percentages of emotions rather than relying on raw counts. To achieve this, the function calc_emotion_percentages() is implemented to filter the dataset by gender, aggregate emotion counts within each subset, and normalize the counts into percentages of total emotional expressions for that gender. This method allows for a fair comparison of emotional distributions while accounting for imbalances in sample sizes between male and female characters.

Plotly is used to create this interactive radar chart, a specialized visualization technique suited for comparing multiple categorical variables across multiple groups. The radar chart represents emotional expression patterns by mapping percentages of emotions along the radius and aligning them with emotion categories on the angular axis. Two traces are added using add_trace(), distinguishing between male and female emotional expressions. Color mapping assigns blue to male characters and red to female characters, with slight transparency to highlight overlapping areas. Interactive hover features are enabled, displaying precise percentages of emotion expression when hovering over data points. Additionally, the maximum percentage value is dynamically computed to ensure appropriate axis scaling. One of the key enhancements of this visualization is its integration into a Shiny framework, allowing users to dynamically filter data. This is achieved through reactive programming, where reactive() ensures that any change in user input automatically updates the radar chart without requiring manual reloading. The implementation of checkboxGroupInput() enables users to select multiple filters, such as playability and specific game titles.

Result

Through the interactive filtering feature in the Shiny application, the audience can explore how gender differences in emotional expression shift based on character type in video games. When the visualization is filtered to display only non-playable characters (NPCs), a notable trend emerges: male NPCs express significantly more anger than female NPCs. This pattern suggests that male NPCs are often written as aggressive, authoritative, or confrontational, whereas female NPCs may be assigned more neutral or passive roles, reinforcing traditional gender norms. In contrast, when the visualization is filtered to display only playable characters (PCs), gender differences in emotional expression shrink considerably. This indicates that game developers tend to invest more effort into creating well-rounded protagonists, ensuring that both male and female PCs exhibit a diverse emotional range rather than conforming to rigid gender stereotypes. The trend becomes even more pronounced when filtering for PCs while excluding games that feature only single-gender protagonists, such as Death Stranding, Disco Elysium, Final Fantasy XV, Hades, Horizon Zero Dawn, and Horizon Forbidden West. In these cases, gender disparities in emotional expression diminish further, suggesting that games with diverse PC lineups are more likely to balance emotional representation across genders. This may reflect a deliberate design choice aimed at enhancing inclusivity and relatability in storytelling.

The visualization remains truthful by maintaining accurate proportion-based comparisons. Normalization ensures that each gender’s emotional distribution is fairly represented, avoiding misinterpretations that might arise from uneven character counts. The Shiny application enhances the visualization’s functions by allowing users to customize the dataset dynamically. By filtering based on character roles and specific game titles, users can uncover patterns that may not be immediately apparent in a single static plot. The radar charts provide an intuitive visual representation, offering a clear and overlapping comparison of male and female emotional expression. The distinct color scheme ensures immediate differentiation, while transparent overlays highlight areas of significant disparity. The radial grid structure enhances readability, making it easier to compare proportions across different emotions. The findings from this visualization offer compelling insights into a hierarchical pattern of emotional gender differences influenced by narrative importance and player interaction. The most striking revelation is that as character agency increases—from NPCs to PCs to mixed-gender PC groups—gender disparities in emotional expression shrink. This suggests that while game developers exercise greater control over gender biases in main characters, NPCs remain more susceptible to stereotypical portrayals. This observation provides quantitative backing for concerns about gender representation in media, demonstrating how game design choices, whether intentional or unconscious, can either reinforce or challenge traditional gender norms.

Visualization 3: Frequency of Gendered Verbs

Method

To clean the dataset storing verbs parsed from video game dialogues, regular expressions are applied to remove non-alphabetical characters and empty values. Once the verbs are properly extracted, the dataset is grouped by verb and gender, and frequency counts are computed to analyze usage patterns. Each verb’s count for each gender is converted into a proportion. A gender ratio is then calculated by comparing these proportions between male and female characters. If a verb is used more frequently by male characters, the ratio is positive; if it is more common among female characters, the ratio is negative. This transformation helps highlight disparities in verb usage while accounting for differences in sample sizez. To maintain focus on the most relevant data, only the top 300 most frequently used verbs are selected. Additionally, a separate subset of the dataset is created to isolate verbs associated with violence and anger, ensuring a more focused analysis of gendered differences in these emotionally charged actions.

The visualization is generated using Plotly, incorporating two distinct traces. General verbs are represented by scatter points, with circular markers color-scaled based on gender tendency. In contrast, violent verbs are displayed as red text labels. The x-axis represents the gender ratio, while the y-axis represents the total frequency of each verb, with both axes log-transformed to mitigate skewness. To improve readability, some y-axis values for violent verbs are slightly adjusted to prevent text overlap. Additionally, hover features dynamically display the verb name and its count upon interaction.

Result

This scatterplot illustrates how verb usage differs between male and female characters in video game dialogue, particularly emphasizing the association of violent and aggressive verbs with male characters. The x-axis represents the gender ratio in verb usage, where verbs positioned on the left are more frequently used by male characters, those on the right are more characteristic of female characters, and verbs near the center are used equally by both genders. A striking pattern emerges: most verbs associated with anger and violence cluster on the male-dominated side of the plot, suggesting that male characters are more often portrayed as aggressive, combative, or forceful in dialogue. This trend highlights a gendered distinction in how characters express themselves through action-related language, reinforcing traditional portrayals of masculinity as assertive or dominant.

The visualization remains truthful, as gender ratio ensures that disparities in verb usage are accurately represented. The scatterplot format is highly functional, allowing viewers to quickly identify overarching trends as well as outlier verbs. Interactive hover features further enhance exploration, enabling users to dynamically inspect specific verb counts and their relative usage between male and female characters. Visually, the plot is clear and well-structured. Color-coded markers enhance readability, making it easy to distinguish different verb tendencies. The use of red labels for violent verbs effectively draws attention to the central finding—the linguistic association between masculinity and aggression in video game dialogue. The plot’s vertical symmetry reinforces the contrast between verbs associated with male and female characters, while the equal-proportion line serves as a neutral baseline, helping viewers interpret deviations in verb usage. Beyond quantifying linguistic patterns, this visualization provides a critical lens on gendered language biases in video game narratives. The skewed distribution of violent verbs toward male characters suggests broader industry trends in character creation, where male characters are more often framed as aggressors, while female characters are distanced from conflict-driven language. These insights contribute to larger discussions on gender representation in media, prompting reflection on how game development shapes player perceptions of gender, power, and action in interactive storytelling.
